# Tacit Knowledge & Accelerated Expertise (Commoncog Method)

> The most valuable expertise is tacit — it can't be written down, only learned through imitation, apprenticeship, and deliberate exposure to cases.

**Author:** Cedric Chin, founder of Commoncog (commoncog.com)
**Source:** Commoncog "Becoming Data Driven from First Principles" (2023) and "Business Expertise" series; draws on Gary Klein's NDM and Lia DiBello's Triad Model
**Field-tested:** Applied with operators and consultants; framework behind Stripe Press's docuseries on expertise development

## When to use this
- Trying to learn a skill that resists books and frameworks
- Extracting how an expert actually thinks, not just what they advise
- Designing an apprenticeship or deliberate-practice plan
- Understanding why a domain feels un-teachable
- Deciding where AI can vs. cannot substitute for human pattern recognition

## The framework
Three-stage expertise development:
1. **Conscious Incompetence** — You know you don't know
2. **Conscious Competence** — You can do it, but it requires effort
3. **Unconscious Competence** — You've internalized it; it "feels right"

**Chin's key insight:** When an expert gives a long list of caveats, you're watching tacit knowledge in action. Frameworks articulate the surface; the real value is the unarticulated pattern-matching in the expert's head.

**For AI use:** AI excels at the Conscious Competence tier (applying explicit frameworks) but cannot substitute for the Unconscious Competence of domain pattern recognition.

## How to run it (step by step)
1. Identify true experts in your field
2. Study not just *what* they do but *how they think* about decisions
3. Use cognitive task analysis: ask "what did you notice?" and "what were you thinking when…?" rather than asking for advice
4. Seek deliberate exposure to many real cases; imitate before you theorize
5. Track which stage you're in for each sub-skill and target the gaps

## Facilitating with the user
When this skill activates, you (Claude) should:
1. Restate or gather the user's context (the skill/domain, available experts, current stage)
2. Walk through each step of the framework sequentially, applied to their situation
3. Make any filled-in gaps explicit and labeled as assumptions
4. Flag where the learning is weakest — usually the un-articulated tacit layer you can't supply
5. Suggest 1-2 complementary frameworks for a second pass (e.g., deliberate-practice planning, OKRs to track progress)

## Output template
- **Target skill & current stage:** <conscious incompetence / competence / unconscious competence>
- **Experts to study:** <names/roles + what to observe about their thinking>
- **Cognitive task analysis questions:** <specific "what did you notice / think" prompts>
- **Case exposure plan:** <sources of real cases to imitate and review>
- **Where AI helps vs. can't:** <explicit-framework tasks vs. tacit pattern recognition>

## Watch out for
- Mistaking articulated frameworks for the real (tacit) expertise
- Asking experts for advice instead of probing how they decide
- Skipping case exposure and over-theorizing
- Assuming AI can replace domain pattern recognition it has no examples of
