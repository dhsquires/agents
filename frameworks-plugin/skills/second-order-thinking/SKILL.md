# Second-Order Thinking ("And Then What?")

> Most bad decisions come from stopping at the immediate, obvious consequence — keep asking "and then what?"

**Author:** Ray Dalio (Bridgewater Associates); framing derived from systems thinking and Howard Marks (Oaktree Capital)
**Source:** *Principles* — Ray Dalio (Simon & Schuster, 2017); Howard Marks, *The Most Important Thing* (Columbia University Press, 2011)
**Field-tested:** Bridgewater's investment process; used by Howard Marks to construct contrarian positions in bond markets; standard in systems thinking and policy analysis

## When to use this
- Evaluating a decision that looks good in the short term
- Checking for unintended or delayed consequences
- Stress-testing a choice that confirms your existing bias
- Building a contrarian or non-obvious position
- Policy, investment, or systems decisions with ripple effects

## The framework
- **First-order thinking** — "If I do X, what happens immediately?"
- **Second-order thinking** — "...and then what? And if that happens, then what?"
- Continue cascading: third, fourth-order consequences

"Failing to consider second- and third-order consequences is the cause of a lot of painfully bad decisions, and it is especially deadly when the first inferior option confirms your own biases." — Ray Dalio

## How to run it (step by step)
1. State the decision or action
2. List all first-order consequences (immediate, obvious)
3. For each first-order consequence, ask "And then what?"
4. Repeat for 2–3 rounds
5. Look for second-order consequences that contradict or undermine the first-order benefits

## Facilitating with the user
When this skill activates, you (Claude) should:
1. Restate or gather the user's context (the decision and the apparent first-order benefit)
2. Walk through each order of consequence sequentially
3. Make any filled-in gaps explicit and labeled as assumptions
4. Flag the step where the analysis is weakest or you lack data
5. Suggest 1-2 complementary frameworks for a second pass

## Output template
- **Decision:** the action under consideration
- **First-order consequences:** immediate, obvious effects
- **Second-order:** for each first-order effect, "and then what?"
- **Third-order:** one level deeper
- **Most dangerous consequence:** the effect you may be ignoring that undermines the apparent benefit

## Watch out for
- Stopping at first-order effects because they confirm your bias
- Cascading only the favorable branches and ignoring the harmful ones
- Going so many levels deep the analysis becomes speculative noise
- Ignoring time delays — second-order effects often arrive later
