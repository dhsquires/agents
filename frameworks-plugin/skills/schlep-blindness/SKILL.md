# Schlep Blindness

> Your unconscious won't let you see opportunities that involve painful, tedious execution — but that schlep is exactly where the defensibility lives.

**Author:** Paul Graham, Co-Founder of Y Combinator
**Source:** "Schlep Blindness" essay (paulgraham.com, 2012)
**Field-tested:** Y Combinator has funded hundreds of schlep-heavy companies; Stripe (payment integrations), Airbnb (regulation + trust), Scale AI (data labeling) all won in overlooked, difficult areas

## When to use this
- Searching for startup or product ideas
- Hunting for a durable moat / defensibility
- Suspecting you're dismissing opportunities because they look like too much work
- Evaluating "boring" or unglamorous problem spaces
- Choosing between an easy idea and a hard, defensible one

## The framework
Your unconscious hides ideas that involve painful, tedious execution. The most valuable opportunities often look like too much work.

The antidote — the **"Someone Else Test"**: instead of asking "What should I build/pursue?", ask "What do I wish *someone else* would solve for me?"

**The moat insight:** The schlep is the defensibility. Work that feels heavy to you also feels heavy to competitors.

**Application:** The hardest integration problems (messy SOAP APIs, legacy connectors) are often the most defensible once solved.

## How to run it (step by step)
1. Name the domain or problem space in scope
2. Apply the Someone Else Test — what do you wish someone else would solve for you?
3. List the schleps you've been unconsciously avoiding because they seem tedious
4. For each schlep, evaluate: (1) real demand, (2) defensibility as a moat, (3) feasibility given current skills
5. Prioritize the schlep with strong demand and durable defensibility

## Facilitating with the user
When this skill activates, you (Claude) should:
1. Restate or gather the user's context (their domain, skills, and what problems surround them)
2. Walk through each step of the framework sequentially, applied to their situation
3. Make any filled-in gaps explicit and labeled as assumptions
4. Flag the step where the analysis is weakest or you lack data (often real-demand evidence)
5. Suggest 1-2 complementary frameworks for a second pass (e.g., PR/FAQ to validate, OKRs to scope execution)

## Output template
- **Domain:** <problem space>
- **Someone Else Test answers:** <what you wish someone solved for you>
- **Avoided schleps:** <tedious opportunities surfaced>
- **Evaluation table:** for each schlep — demand / defensibility / feasibility
- **Recommended pursuit:** <the schlep worth doing + why it's a moat>

## Watch out for
- Confirmation bias toward easy, glamorous ideas — those have the least defensibility
- Mistaking "hard for everyone" (a moat) for "hard for no good reason" (just pain)
- Skipping real-demand validation — a defensible schlep nobody wants is still worthless
- Underestimating the stamina required; schleps are, by definition, grinding
